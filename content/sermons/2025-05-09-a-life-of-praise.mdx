Psalm 145 is David's last recorded song, an acrostic of God's greatness from A to Z. The message follows that praise into five claims of worth — time, testimony, loyalty, trust, and refuge — and lands on a quiet response: be still. There was no livestream that day; the manuscript is the message.

## Opening

We had just sung about the goodness and faithfulness of God — how He has been running after us with mercy all our lives. If we really believe those words, the natural question is: how should we respond to a God like that? With a life of praise.

Psalm 145 is the final recorded psalm of King David. Most scholars place it late in his life, after years of seeing God's faithfulness in both victory and failure — the heights of triumph and the depths of sin and family heartbreak. Reflecting back, David does not turn inward. He turns upward, praising the greatness and goodness of God.

This is also the only psalm explicitly titled "A Song of Praise." Every psalm that follows begins and ends with Hallelujah — a prelude to the Hallelujah chorus. It is written as an acrostic: each verse begins with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet. Of the eight acrostic psalms, this one stands out as if David is trying to cover the whole range of who God is from A to Z.

Why are we here in this text? Because we need a reset — a re-centering of the heart. Some of us walk in carrying despair or anxiety and need to learn to hope again. Others, if we are honest, are battling apathy — a slow drift into spiritual numbness, where familiarity with the things of God no longer produces awe.

This psalm speaks to that. It confronts despair with hope, anxiety with God's sustaining presence, and apathy with renewed awe of His greatness. It teaches God-centered optimism, not naïve optimism.

As Paul Tripp writes in *Dangerous Calling*: "Every human being has been hardwired by God to live in daily awe of God." When we lose that awe, it affects every part of life — decisions, worship, even relationships.

The prayer of this message is that the Spirit would send despair, anxiety, and apathy packing, and replace them with a fresh vision of God's majesty and mercy — because when you truly see who He is, the only right response is a life of praise.

## He is worthy of my time (vv. 1–3)

A life of praise begins here: it recognizes that God is worthy of my time.

Verses 1–3 open with a bold "I will." David takes action. These are not passive thoughts — they are intentional choices to lift high the name of God. "I will exalt you… I will praise your name… I will bless you every day." This is the language of someone who has decided that God is worthy of his time, his voice, and his life.

But is Jesus your King — not only in words, but in reality? He is only your King if you have bowed your life to Him, submitted your will, and said, "You lead, I follow."

Psalm 145 is part of a daily rhythm of praise in Jewish life known as the Ashrei, recited three times a day. Those words are reminders to align life around the greatness and goodness of God — alongside the pilgrimage heart of Psalm 84:5, the blessing of Psalm 144:15 ("Blessed are the people whose God is the Lord"), and the forever praise of Psalm 115:18.

You may notice the word *my*. Our hearts often approach God as if time were ours to spare: "I have this time, but I'd rather do that with it." The truth is, it is not my time. Psalm 90:12 says, "Teach us to number our days, that we may gain a heart of wisdom." Every moment is a gift, not a possession. God is the author of time; we are stewards of it.

So what are we meant to do with the time He gives? John 15:4 answers: "Abide in me, and I in you." That word *abide* — Greek *meno* — means to remain, to dwell, to make your home there. Abiding is not rushing through. It is setting up camp with Jesus and staying — fully present, fully surrendered, fully His. Maybe today you need Do Not Disturb not only on your phone, but on your life, and just be with Jesus.

## He is worthy of my testimony (vv. 4–9)

If the first response is to give God our time, the second follows: He is worthy of my testimony. When you have spent time with God — when you have truly abided — you cannot help but talk about Him. David moves from "I will exalt You" to "One generation shall commend Your works to another." Time with God should overflow into your story about God. The world does not only need people who know theology; it needs people who have walked with God and can say, "Let me tell you what He's done for me."

Romans 10:14 presses the same reality: how will they believe in Him of whom they have never heard? Your story matters. The gospel lived out in your life matters. The God who is unsearchably great invites you not only to know Him, but to make Him known. And it is not about a perfect testimony — it is about pointing to a perfect Savior.

In verse 5 David says, "On the glorious splendor of your majesty, and on your wondrous works, I will meditate." You cannot pray or worship with that depth if you have grown apathetic to God's majesty. If the heart is dull, praise will be shallow — and the next generation is watching.

Verses 6–7 paint how it is supposed to look: they speak of His awesome deeds, pour forth the fame of His abundant goodness, and sing aloud of His righteousness. That is generational discipleship — one generation testifying to the next, not only with information, but with celebration. Revelation 7:9–12 shows where that song leads: every tribe and tongue around the throne. Where do they learn to sing like that? From faithful believers passing on the greatness of God.

So what are you passing on? The saving activity of God — deliverance after deliverance through Scripture, and His ultimate act: saving you through the death and resurrection of Jesus. The next generation needs to taste and see that the Lord is good (Psalm 34:8). If we are more passionate about a touchdown than the cross, we are teaching them something — just not what matters most.

We cannot pass anything on without meditating on the gospel — not only hearing it, but sitting with it until it stirs us again. Discipleship is not duplication of behavior; it is the overflow of a heart captured by grace. Reproduce and share the load for His glory. Protect your time with Jesus. Deuteronomy 6:4–7, Psalm 78:5–7, and Ephesians 6:4 place that call first in the home. Church leaders may get an hour or two a week with your kids; you get the rest. Our words may say Jesus is King — but our emotions may tell them otherwise.

And if you have forgotten how good our God really is, look at verse 9: "The Lord is good to all, and his mercy is over all that he has made." Tony Merida notes that God's common grace extends even to the wicked for a vapor of a lifetime — while those in Christ will enjoy His goodness forever. Do not waste the opportunity. You still have breath. When the Pharisees wanted to silence the praises of Jesus entering Jerusalem, He said the stones would cry out (Luke 19:40). Let your life and testimony be why someone sees and savors the goodness of God.

## He is worthy of my loyalty (vv. 10–13a)

Verse 10 says everything God does leads to thanks. His saints speak boldly of His kingdom, tell of His power, and pass on the splendor of that kingdom (vv. 11–12).

But where is our allegiance? Many of us have no trouble naming who our God is. Living like He is King is another matter. Are we loyal to His kingdom — and how much?

God's kingdom is not only a far-off future. Psalm 103:19 says His kingdom rules over all. Even Nebuchadnezzar, after being humbled, declared that God's kingdom is an eternal kingdom (Daniel 4:3). God does not need our loyalty to prove His reign; He will be exalted. The difference is whether we willingly submit or are humbled into recognizing His rule. Jesus made it personal: "My kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36) and "Repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (Matthew 4:17). The kingdom is His rule over every heart that surrenders to Him.

Have you surrendered? Where does your allegiance truly lie? Is something else demanding loyalty ahead of God? David makes clear: loyalty belongs with the Lord and His kingdom — with Him seated on the throne of the heart.

## He is worthy of my trust (vv. 13b–17)

In the remaining verses David highlights a profound truth: our God is worthy of trust because He is actively involved in the details of our lives.

Verse 14 shows that God's care is not limited by our weakness. Weakness is not a limitation but an opportunity to experience His grace more fully — humble dependence, trusting His strength rather than our own.

Verses 15–16 paint God's provision: every creature looks to the Creator. Jesus echoed this in the Sermon on the Mount — if God cares for birds and flowers, how much more for us. Martin Luther put it this way: "Let the birds become your teachers, and the flowers your theologians." God's provision is always available; He will never fail us. He has proven Himself trustworthy again and again.

Have you forgotten His faithfulness because of the valley you find yourself in?

## He is worthy because He is my refuge (vv. 18–20)

God is worthy because He is our refuge — not only a place to hide when things go wrong, but a place to run in every circumstance. Always near, always ready to listen, always able to save.

Verses 18–19 show that God is near to those who call on Him in truth. It is not enough to pray while secretly trusting human wisdom or material wealth. As Tony Merida writes, "Don't cry out to the Lord for help but secretly trust in something else." God desires undivided trust.

Verse 19 reassures us that God fulfills the desires of those who fear Him; He hears their cry and saves them. Through humility we enter intimate relationship with Him. Jesus echoes this in Luke 14:11 — those who humble themselves will be lifted up. Calling on God is a recognition of need; in that place He draws near.

Verse 20 emphasizes that God preserves those who love Him — sustaining, maintaining, upholding His people — while the wicked face destruction.

Have you made God your refuge? Do you turn to Him with sincerity, trusting His provision, loyalty, and care? He is worthy of your time, your testimony, your loyalty, your trust — and above all, of being your refuge in every season.

## Closing: be still

David ends the psalm the way he began — with praise. Psalm 145:21: "My mouth will speak the praise of the Lord, and let all flesh bless his holy name forever and ever." This is David near the end of his life — not a young man full of optimism, but an older man full of experience. After highs and lows, victory and failure, sin and restoration, he still says: God is worthy of praise.

Our God is worthy — of my time, my testimony, my loyalty, my trust, and ultimately my refuge. How do we get back to, or begin, a life of praise?

It starts with confession. Romans 10:9: if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord and believe in your heart that God raised Him from the dead, you will be saved. That is the beginning of praise: surrender to the One who can save your soul.

For those already following Christ, the invitation is simpler: **be still**.

You do not need a checklist of next steps. You need to pause. We live in constant motion. When we are not busy, we find ways to become busy. What are we trying to escape? Often it is violence against the soul we would rather bury than bring to healing.

The Chinese character for "busy" (*máng*) joins the radicals for "heart" and "perish" — literally "heart perish." Jason Wilson puts it bluntly in *Battle Cry*: "Rest now or rest in peace."

We must learn to be still before God. When we are still, He reveals what is really going on inside — the obvious things others can see, and the hidden things: anxiety, fear, unbelief, and sin. Not to shame us, but to heal us.

Tony Merida again: if you feel too afflicted, too sinful, or too poor to cry out for help, think again — you are a great candidate for the King's grace. And Stephen Smith in *Soul Custody*: we might not quake in God's presence because we will not sit still long enough to give Him a chance. More information does not yield a transformed life. Transformation is ushered in by experiencing God — and stillness is required.

So the action response is this: be still. Go outside. Take a walk and talk to God. Sit with your Bible and read Psalm 145 again. Let these truths about who God is sink in. Then maybe tomorrow, do it again. Let God meet you in the quiet.

Because that is where a life of praise begins.
